# Informational, orienting, and exploratory content

Use this guidance for articles, history and culture pages, public information, reference pages, directories, archives, collection pages, about material whose main job is explanation, and other pages that do not primarily sell.

## Inform

Lead with the clearest accurate answer to the visitor's likely question. Then add context in the order needed to understand it.

- State scope, place, time, and definitions early when they affect meaning.
- Separate chronology from cause. Events occurring in sequence do not prove one caused another.
- Separate primary evidence, secondary interpretation, and local tradition or disputed accounts.
- Prefer literal headings that name the subject or finding.
- Use concrete examples to make an abstract point understandable, not to substitute for evidence.
- Link to sources or further reading where a visitor may want to verify or continue.

Do not inject promotional language merely because the content appears on a website. An article about a town's history needs an accurate subject and useful framing, not a promise to transform the reader.

## Orient

Orientation content reduces uncertainty about location, scope, and routes.

- Name the current section and what belongs there.
- Group destinations by the visitor's mental model rather than the site's org chart.
- Use navigation labels that predict the destination.
- Make the most common route visible without hiding alternate routes.
- Explain access limits, prerequisites, or dead ends before the visitor encounters them.

## Explore

Exploration supports discovery without pretending every visitor follows one funnel.

- Offer meaningful categories, filters, related items, or curated paths.
- Explain why items are related when the connection is not obvious.
- Let titles and summaries distinguish items from each other.
- Preserve a sense of place or editorial judgment without exaggerating significance.
- Avoid repetitive teaser language that withholds the useful fact to manufacture clicks.

## Editorial shape

Choose a structure that fits the material. Useful options include chronology, question and answer, geographic movement, comparison, thematic clusters, definition followed by implications, or evidence followed by interpretation.

Do not force Hero, Problem, Solution, Proof, and CTA sections onto material whose job is to explain or document.
